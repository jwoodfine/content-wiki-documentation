> The register guide for institutional communications — announcements, correspondence, memos,
> meeting notes, chat, and ticket comments. Builds on [[house-core]]; restates nothing there.
> One register runs across the whole formality range; only the shape changes per genre.

## 1. Purpose and audience

A communication moves a decision, a fact, or a request from the writer to a reader who will act
on it. The reader is busy and reading to answer one question: what happened, or what do I need
to do? Everything in the message serves that question or is cut.

The register is institutional: precise, professional, consequence-first — the opposite of
marketing. It holds constant from a press announcement down to a one-line chat message; what
scales is formality, not register. The second reader is the one this message is forwarded to
without its context — write so the point survives the forward. External audiences may include
readers who rely on the message under disclosure rules, so what is not yet true is framed as
not yet true.

## 2. The shape

One common spine, scaled per genre:

- **Subject / purpose up top** — the reader knows the topic before the first sentence.
- **The point in the first line** — the decision, the fact, or the ask, stated first.
- **Supporting detail after** — context, rationale, and specifics, in descending importance.
- **A clear ask or next step** — who does what by when, or "no action needed" stated plainly.

Per-genre deltas:

- **Announcement / press** — a dated, factual lead; quotes are attributed and add fact, not
  adjectives.
- **Memo** — decision, then rationale, then who-does-what.
- **Email** — one subject; the ask in the first two lines.
- **Meeting notes** — decisions and owners, not a transcript.
- **Chat / ticket comment** — one point, actionable, no throat-clearing.

## 3. Opening

The point goes in sentence one. A communication has no warm-up: the reader learns the outcome,
the decision, or the request before any context arrives. Context earns its place only after the
point has landed.

For an announcement, the lead is a dated statement of fact: what is true, as of when. For a
memo, sentence one is the decision. For an email, the subject line carries the topic and the
first two lines carry the ask. For a note or a comment, the first clause is the whole point.

The isolation test: a reader who reads only the subject and the first line knows what happened
and whether they must act. If the ask is buried below the fold, the opening has failed.

## 4. Paragraph and sentence rhythm

External communications target 14 to 18 words a sentence — tighter than an explanation, because
a reader scanning for the ask should not wade through subordinate clauses. The point-carrying
sentence comes first and stays short.

Internal notes, chat, and ticket comments run shorter still — often a single sentence is the
whole message. Length signals importance, so do not pad a small message to look substantial, and
do not compress a consequential one to look casual.

## 5. Headings and scannability

Short genres carry no headings — an email, a chat message, a ticket comment are too small to
section. A memo or a longer announcement uses a few sentence-case headings: the decision, the
rationale, the actions.

Meeting notes are best as a structure, not prose: a short decisions list and an
owner-and-deadline table beat paragraphs a reader must mine for their name. Use a table when the
recurring content is "who does what by when." Use a list when order does not matter and each
item stands alone.

## 6. Voice and tone

The move this register turns on is the consequence-first sentence: the reader learns the result,
then the reason.

> The provisioning window moves to Thursday; the migration needs one more validation pass.

Result first, cause second, no adjectives doing the work a fact should do. The institutional
tone is built from named facts and dates, not intensifiers — the positive alternative to hype is
always the specific, verifiable claim. In an announcement, a quote adds a fact or a named
commitment; it does not add enthusiasm. Attribute every quote to a role or a named person and
keep it to what that person can stand behind.

**Forward-looking and disclosure posture.** Present facts go in the present tense. Anything not
yet true — a capability, a date, an outcome — carries *planned*, *intended*, *may*, or *target*,
with a reasonable basis. External announcements in regulated contexts carry the cautionary
framing the disclosure posture requires; see [[house-core]] for the base rule. Never state a
target as an accomplishment, and never give a system or the organization an intent it does not
have.

## 7. Code and examples — code policy

Communications generally carry no code. The exception is a ticket comment or a chat message
where a command is the clearest answer — fence it and keep it to the one command, with no
surrounding explanation the reader did not ask for. Longer procedures do not belong in a
message; link to the guide that carries them.

## 8. Worked examples

**Buried ask → lead with the ask.**

> Weaker: Following up on our earlier thread about the staging environment and the various
> validation steps we discussed, I wanted to circle back — could you approve the window?
> Stronger: Please approve the Thursday provisioning window by end of day. Context: the
> migration needs one more validation pass, which pushes us from Wednesday.

*Put the ask and its deadline in the first line; the context that was blocking it now supports
it instead.*

**Hype sentence → dated factual statement.**

> Weaker: We're thrilled to unveil a game-changing platform that redefines what's possible.
> Stronger: On 1 July 2026 the organization released version 2 of the platform, adding
> federated search across all three content repositories.

*Replaced the feeling with a date and a named capability; the reader learns what shipped and
when, which is what an announcement is for.*

**Meeting-notes transcript → decisions and owners.**

> Weaker: We talked for a while about the queue and then discussed whether to move the deadline,
> and there were a few opinions, and eventually it seemed like Thursday made sense.
> Stronger: Decision — provisioning moves to Thursday. Owner — operator. Reason — one more
> validation pass required.

*Kept the decision, the owner, and the reason; dropped the discussion a reader does not need to
re-live.*

## 9. Pre-publish checklist

- Does the reader learn the point from the subject and first line alone?
- Is the ask — or "no action needed" — stated plainly, near the top, with an owner and a date?
- Does every sentence carry a fact, not an intensifier standing in for one?
- Is anything not yet true framed as planned, intended, may, or target?
- Is every quote attributed and carrying a fact rather than enthusiasm?
- Do meeting notes record decisions and owners rather than the discussion?
- Is the message as short as the point allows, and no shorter?
- Does the subject/title lead with its keyword, not a leading article (*the/a/an*)? (see [[house-core]] §Capitalization)
